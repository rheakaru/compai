# The Rhai Sales Playbook

**How to pitch Rhai and our solutions.**
Version 1.0 — derived from 14 client calls, 16–30 August 2026.

This is the working document for anyone taking a Rhai discovery or pitch call. It captures what Rhea actually says that works, in the order she says it, plus the objections that come up and the answers that land. Nothing here is aspirational — every line is drawn from a real call.

---

## Part 1 — What we sell

Rhai has exactly **two offerings**. Say them in this order, every time.

**1. AI workshops.** One- or two-day sessions that get a company's senior management and teams aligned on what to do with AI. Either an introduction, or — for teams already using AI — how to get to the product they actually want, and how to rethink the company in the age of AI.

**2. Tech builds.** Dashboards, ERPs, agents, apps. Most workshops end with a demo dashboard, and most clients then ask us to build that demo into the real thing.

The connective tissue between them, which is the actual pitch: **the workshop is how we find out what's worth building, and it's how we get the team to want it.** We do not sell software into a room that hasn't bought in.

> "I can build any tool, hand it over, charge 20 lakhs and walk away. But if that tool doesn't get used then it's not useful for you, and for me it doesn't give that satisfaction."

### The two-sided credibility we lead with

We are not an AI vendor. We are **operators who built our own systems and then got asked to do it for others**. That distinction is the whole moat and it should never get lost in a pitch:

- Founders/CTO of Hoovu Fresh — seven years, nine cities, 365 days a year, live on Blinkit/BigBasket/Flipkart, Shark Tank.
- Computer science at Stanford; part of the teaching team for intro CS.
- Went back to Stanford last year *because* AI had automated enough of the operating role to make it possible — and saw the gap between how AI is used in Silicon Valley and how it's used at the best Indian companies.
- Came back, ran free workshops, trained 100+ people. Attendees started inviting us into their companies.
- Incorporated July 2026. **12 companies in the first two months.**

## Part 2 — The call spine

This is the running order that works. Deviating from it is what loses deals (see Part 5).

### 0. Frame the call (30 seconds)

> "Let me start out by giving you a little bit of an intro and how we usually do these calls — and then most of the call I'll actually be listening to you, in terms of what [company] is currently doing and what you need."

This does real work. It tells them the call is about them, it buys permission for the credibility block, and it sets up the discovery as the main event.

### 1. Credibility (2 minutes — never skip, never delay)

The Hoovu → Stanford → SF gap → free workshops → invited in → 12 companies arc, above. **Deliver this in the first three minutes of every call, including calls that come through a warm referral.** See Part 5, failure mode 1, for what happens when it comes late.

### 2. The two offerings (30 seconds)

Workshops, then tech builds, then the connective tissue.

### 3. Discovery — most of the call

Stop talking. What you're listening for, in priority order:

1. **The repeated workflow.** The single most valuable thing a client can say is some version of *"we do this every single time, for every project."* At Century that was the product team's competition study — rebuilt from scratch for every launch, presented, then forgotten. When you hear it, name it back immediately:
   > "That's a perfect use case — if it's the same workflow but different information each time."
2. **Where data dies.** Information that exists but isn't reachable: the product team that "alone holds it"; WhatsApp threads with no formal structure; presentations that get made and then forgotten.
3. **Seed terms.** Industry vocabulary dropped in passing. Repeat it back and pull the thread. This is what lets us build something that isn't generic.
4. **Existing stack and existing vendors.** Salesforce, SAP, Tally, Zoho, Quadra, custom ERP. Also: *who else are they already talking to?* Century had already shortlisted 10–12 Salesforce use cases and had their own L&D team running workshops. Knowing that early would have saved half the call.
5. **Who decides, and what's the budget.**

### 4. The demo (10–15 minutes)

Always Hoovu first, then the industry-adjacent demo. The reason to open with Hoovu:

> "It gives you an overall view of what's possible for us to build, because we built a whole suite of things here. And then I can show you the use-case-specific, industry-specific things as well."

The Hoovu walkthrough, in order:

1. **The AI CEO briefing** on the founder front page. Then: *"the best dashboard is a dashboard you don't have to open"* — it arrives as an email every morning.
2. **The AI CFO briefing.** Different agent, because *"the personality of a CFO and their priorities are different from a CEO — and the CFO is allowed to panic a lot more than the AI CEO is."* This line lands in every room.
3. **Chat, gated by login.** Everyone can query the AI CEO; what it reveals depends on who's asking.
4. **Proactive mode — the money moment.** The agent reads the data on a 30-minute cadence and *acts*: sends chats, sends emails, adds tasks to people's dashboards. Note the trust ramp: for the first two to three weeks it only *suggested* actions; once it proved out, it was allowed to take them.
   > "Your dashboard shouldn't just be a report that's making the company legible to you. If it's making the company legible to AI as well, then it should act as an operator."
5. **The context graph** (POLE+O — people, organisation, location, event, object). Show the agent highlighting which nodes it used to answer a question. This is the visibility/trust argument, and it's also the answer to any "knowledge base / RAG" requirement.
6. **North Stars.** Company- and department-level goals built into the dashboard, so the agent has direction, not just history.
7. **World model + real-world signal.** The Dorsey frame:
   > "Even if your model starts low, if your signal is good enough, over time it improves. But if you build the best model on day one and the signal isn't there, it stagnates or gets worse."
   Then the concrete example: meeting transcripts flow in as signal, become tasks, and the AI CEO follows up — *"instead of us assigning work on Monday, chasing it Wednesday to Friday, and yelling at people on the weekend."*
8. **The sales page and the warehouse page.** Different personas, radically different layouts. This sets up the deployment argument.

Then the industry demo — Concord's lead qualifier, Mantri's tenant billing, Renaissance's land-lead qualifier, Dodla, SRC's field app. **Always say "this is synthetic data"** — it protects existing clients and it demonstrates the NDA discipline we're about to ask them to sign.

### 5. The deployment argument — our actual differentiator

Say this explicitly. It's the strongest thing we have and it does not come across on its own:

> "This technology already exists. I'm not the one creating the LLM — we're all using Claude or ChatGPT or Gemini. They're a dime a dozen. The one thing we bring is that, having been entrepreneurs and having made 200 garlanding ladies use this platform even though they're not comfortable with laptops, we know that deployment is where you make or break a technology system."

Follow it with the two-level adoption point:

> "When I'm pitching to executives, I know your problem statement — you want the important metrics and you want to drill down. But you only get that data if information is moving *upward* through the organisation. So I also have to solve a problem for each person at that level, so they'll use the dashboard day to day."

Sales logs are the standard example: nobody wants to sit at a computer after a meeting, so we take voice notes and structure them on the backend.

### 6. The process (2 minutes)

Clients buy this as much as the product, because it de-risks them. Walk it in order:

1. **Discovery** — understand the problem, collect documents.
2. **Proposal + demo dashboard** — built from their documents, with costing included.
3. **Spec** — a written PDF of every page we'll build. They sign off before we build.
4. **Three feedback rounds.** *At least one on site — preference is all three.* Reasoning to say out loud: *"your corporate feedback should be coming to me at the spec level. The feedback rounds are for getting it used by your team."*
5. **Handover** — a week on site making sure it's actually being used, then a one-week check-in and a one-month check-in.
6. **Ownership decision** — keys or AMC (Part 3).

Timeline: **four weeks.** Say why it's four and not less: *"we usually get it much faster, but you have three edit cycles, so it depends on your feedback timing."*

### 7. Ask for the budget — always

> "I do want to ask you this, and feel free to tell me if you don't want to answer — do you have a budget in mind? So I know how to scope it out."

This works. Century answered "not more than 10 lakhs," which immediately made the project scopeable, and Rhea closed the loop honestly: *"in 10 lakhs we can get a really good product, and then we don't have to add a lot of bells and whistles."* When a client has no figure (Halol), give them the tier ladder instead and let them locate themselves on it.

### 8. Close

NDA (offer it proactively — *"we sign one whether we go forward or not"*), the specific documents we need, the named next meeting, and who else should be in the room.

## Part 3 — Pricing

### Workshops

| Tier | Price | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Intro | ₹1 lakh | Three-hour session, not personalised |
| Personalised | ₹3 lakhs | Full day, personalised after a discovery call |
| Personalised + demo | ₹5 lakhs | Full day, personalised, **and we build the demo product** |

Rhea's own framing on the top tier: the demo build is *"honestly the most valuable part of what we do."* Push toward it.

### Tech builds

| Scope | Price | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Consulting only | ~₹5 lakhs | We advise their existing team, build nothing but a demo |
| Small scoped build | ₹5–7 lakhs | With WhatsApp and similar tools at the top of that range |
| AI layer on an existing ERP | ₹10–12 lakhs | Firm number after we've worked with their ERP team |
| Full dashboard / end-to-end build | ₹15–25 lakhs | Drivers below |

What moves a build from ₹15L to ₹25L, in the order we explain it:
- **Number of data sources** we're connecting to
- **An Android app** — always more expensive, because AI is weaker at building it and Rhea builds it by hand
- **External APIs** (Google and similar) push toward ₹20L+
- **Number of pages** — Dodla was ~70 pages, which is the ₹25L end

Always anchor against the market:
> "Most of these projects are 50 to 60 lakhs. The other parameter where we're reasonable is time — you save a lot in your own time and your feedback cycles."

**⚠️ Fix needed:** in the last two weeks the full-build range was quoted as "15 to 25 lakhs" (Century) and "20 to 25 lakhs" (Halol) three working days apart. Pick one and put it in the proposal template. Recommendation: **₹15–25 lakhs**, with the drivers above used to place a client inside the range.

### Ownership: keys vs AMC

Present the keys model first — it's the honest one and it builds enormous trust:

> "I want to show your team how to use Claude Code to take it further. I give them the Firebase hosting and database keys and the Anthropic keys, and then it's their project. Then there's no AMC from my side."

Then the AMC options, if they don't have anyone internal who wants to own it: monthly retainer, or per-call charge. Rhea prefers per-call, and the reasoning is the differentiator:

> "The whole difference with building with AI is that it's adaptable and you can extend it yourself. You can add attributes, change priorities, chat with the agent to train it, upload documents to change its behaviour. If I build this well, you won't need me for every minor edit — only for a major feature."

Note that some large clients *want* an AMC — Mantri asked for a minimum one-year. Don't talk them out of it; just don't lead with it.

## Part 4 — Objections and the answers that land

### "Isn't this just vibe coding? Won't it break in a few years?"

**The single most important objection we face.** It killed a deal in this period. Answer it early and directly:

- "You're 100% right that it can't be vibe coding — hosting, database, cost all have to be looked at."
- Stanford CS; taught intro CS; built the entire Hoovu platform over seven years.
- "We use AI to speed up development, but we also use AI *within* the product."
- The client proof: *"Dodla is a listed company, ₹4,000 crore, 8,000 people on the field. They're not going to use a vibe-coded solution — security matters and they can't afford something that doesn't work accurately. Whatever we build is enterprise grade."*

**Pre-empt this with any technical buyer.** Don't wait to be asked.

### "You don't know our industry."

> "I haven't found industry specificity to be a barrier — not because I'm an expert, but because AI is the expert."

Then the mechanism: when the NDA goes out with their legal name, we run background and industry research; we learn the vocabulary (LPD, NFP for dairy) that's essential to building the right solution. Then the proof list — dairy, commercial real estate and retail, government infrastructure, all industry-specific, all built end to end.

### "Should AI be built into the ERP, or on top of an existing one?"

The cleanest explanation we have. Use it verbatim:

> "What's difficult about building an ERP? Taking the chaotic world and putting it in a structured format. What's difficult about applying an AI agent to your business? Anyone can open Claude and ask a question, but Claude doesn't understand your company. So you need to take your big chaotic company and put it in a structured format — and code is the most structured format there is.
>
> When I build an agent on top of your existing ERP, I am reading through your entire ERP and recreating it in my system as a structure for the AI to read. So whether I'm building your ERP with the AI in it, or reading your existing ERP and explaining it to my AI — either way it has to know the whole ERP."

Conclusion to state plainly: native is genuinely better ("100%, yes"), but on-top works to a good degree of functionality. Then offer **both as parallel proposals** rather than arguing for one. That's what kept Halol open.

### "Which model do you use? Can you use open-source / local?"

> "That's a design decision further down the line. Whether it's edge computing, a local LLM, Anthropic, Gemini or OpenAI — as long as our world model is built well enough, any of them works within it. It's swapping out one key."

Personal preference is Claude (best frontier model). Some clients have security requirements that push to local models; we've done that. Firebase is the hosting default — SOC 2, secure, one Google login for their team to manage — with AWS when the client requires it (Dodla did).

> "For corporate apps you're not likely to cross 10,000 users. AWS is more cost-effective at scale, but the complexity of setting it up isn't worth the friction cost at the beginning."

### "Our team won't use it / our team resists AI"

This is the deployment argument (Part 2, section 5), plus the champion strategy: run the workshop so the team feels like **co-authors** of whatever we build.

> "We start from a team-first approach so we get buy-in — everyone feels like they're a co-author of whatever software we build, and they become the champion for it."

For large multi-division companies, add the pilot framing that worked at Hester and Ananya: **start in one division** — ideally the fastest-growing one — prove value, then expand.

### "We're already doing this internally"

Be honest and fast about it — and then reframe once, properly:

> "It's not about that one use case or that one demo dashboard. It's about getting everyone in the team to start using AI for their specific roles. HR, procurement, every journalist, editor, designer, the social media team — each person has different use cases."

If they still have no gap, park it cleanly and leave the door open. Don't push. But see failure mode 3 below — by the time you're having this conversation, something has usually already gone wrong earlier in the call.

## Part 5 — What's going wrong (and how to fix it)

Five failure modes, each seen in this two-week window.

### 1. Credibility arrives too late on referred calls

**What happened:** On the V6 call, the client's technical lead spent twenty minutes probing whether we were "vibe coders." Rhea's actual credentials — Stanford CS, the intro-CS teaching team, seven years building Hoovu's platform, listed enterprise clients — only came out at minute 18, *after* he'd already said he'd build it internally. She had assumed the referrer (a family friend) had briefed them. They hadn't.

**Fix:** The credibility block goes in the first three minutes of **every** call. Never assume the referrer briefed them. Warm intros get you the meeting; they don't transfer credibility.

### 2. Demoing before discovering

**What happened:** The V6 demo dashboard was built against one use case the client had already built internally. That anchored the entire conversation on a duplicated feature and made everything else we do look redundant.

**Fix:** Discovery first, then demo the thing that maps to what they just told you. If you must demo early, demo **Hoovu** — it shows breadth and can't be "already built internally."

### 3. Not asking who else they're already working with

**What happened:** Century had already evaluated many AI vendors, shortlisted 10–12 Salesforce use cases, engaged Salesforce-recommended implementation partners, and had their own L&D team running a four-week internal workshop series for 30–35 leaders. The workshop offering was dead on arrival and we spent call time on it. What actually converted was the one thing nobody else was covering: a repeated product-team workflow living outside Salesforce.

**Fix:** Ask in discovery, every time: *"Who else are you working with on this? What have you already got in flight?"* Then pitch into the gap, not over the top of it. Rhea's recovery line is a good one to keep:
> "Is there any particular use case you're still open to looking for solutions for, where I could come in? Or do you feel like you've already locked in what you want to build?"

### 4. Inconsistent pricing between calls

Two different full-build ranges quoted three days apart (Part 3). With clients increasingly connected — real estate developers in one city all know each other — this is a live risk. Lock the numbers in a single proposal template.

### 5. Connection quality is costing us calls

Both the V6 and Halol calls had repeated audio dropouts, screen-share failures and a power cut mid-demo. On the V6 call the client had to ask "hello?" eleven times. This is not a small thing when we're selling *technical competence*.

**Fix:** hardwired connection or a mobile hotspot backup for every client call; pre-loaded demo tabs so a share can be restarted in seconds; a recorded demo video as fallback (we already send these — have them ready *during* the call, not after). Never take a pitch call from a car or an area with known power issues.

## Part 6 — Qualifying: who's a good fit

**Strong fit — pursue hard:**
- Family-run or promoter-led company, ₹500 cr+, with a generational transition happening
- Existing ERP that produces "generalised reports" nobody acts on
- A **repeated workflow** rebuilt from scratch every time
- Field or operational staff who won't use laptops (this is where our deployment edge is decisive)
- A named champion inside the company who wants this to happen
- Data that exists but is stuck in WhatsApp, Excel, presentations, or one person's head

**Weak fit — qualify out fast:**
- Strong internal tech team already shipping AI tools (V6)
- Locked into a single-platform strategy with an incumbent partner already engaged (Century's Salesforce work — though the *gap* around it was still winnable)
- No named business problem, just curiosity about AI
- Nobody senior in the room and no route to them

**The qualifying question that decides it:**
> "Is there a gap between the problems you want to solve and the AI capabilities you currently have?"

If there's no gap, say so and park it. We have twelve clients in two months; walking away from a bad fit is affordable and it protects the referral network.
