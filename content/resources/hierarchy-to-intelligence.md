## The core idea

For two thousand years, companies have coordinated work with **hierarchy** — layers of managers whose real job is *routing information*: taking what's happening on the ground, summarising it upward, and pushing decisions back down. The essay traces this from the Roman army's span of control, through the Prussian general staff (who *invented* middle management in 1806), the American railroads (who drew the first org chart), Taylorism, and matrix orgs — to the flat-structure experiments (Spotify, Zappos, Valve) that all quietly reverted to hierarchy as they scaled.

The constant, unsolvable tradeoff: *a narrower span of control means more layers, and more layers mean slower information flow.* Nothing was ever powerful enough to replace hierarchy as the coordination mechanism.

**Block's claim: AI is the first thing that can actually do hierarchy's job** — continuously maintain a live model of what's happening, and coordinate work — without the human middle layer.

## The framework worth stealing

Block rebuilds the company around four layers instead of product teams. This is a genuinely useful lens for how we think about a client's intelligence dashboard:

1. **Capabilities** — the atomic building blocks (for them: payments, lending, payroll). Hard to acquire, reliable, no UI of their own.
2. **World models** — a machine-readable, continuously-updated picture of two things: how the *company* actually operates, and the *customer's* reality. This is the "honest signal" that replaces a manager summarising a spreadsheet.
3. **Intelligence layer** — composes capabilities into *proactive* solutions. Their example: spotting a business's cash flow tightening before a seasonal dip and automatically surfacing a short-term loan. (This is exactly the "notices problems before they escalate, and drafts the response" move we sell.)
4. **Interfaces** — the surfaces the solution reaches people through.

And a three-role structure once the information-routing manager is gone: deep **individual contributors**, **DRIs** (directly responsible individuals) who own a cross-cutting problem with authority to pull resources, and **player-coaches** who build *and* grow people.

## The two lines to remember

- *"Speed is the best predictor of start-up success"* — and speed is really about the quality of information flow. That's the thing hierarchy taxes and an intelligence layer frees.
- The question the essay says every company must answer, which is also the best discovery question we could ask a client: **"What does your company understand that is genuinely hard to understand — and is that understanding getting deeper every day?"**

## What it means for us

When we build a client a demo dashboard, the trap is to build a *prettier status report*. This essay is the argument for the harder, more valuable thing: **an actionable world model** that composes the company's capabilities into decisions automatically, and reserves the humans for the edge — the judgment calls, the ethics, the high-stakes moments the system shouldn't make alone. That distinction — report vs. operator, augmentation vs. replacing the bottleneck — *is* our pitch. This is where it comes from.
